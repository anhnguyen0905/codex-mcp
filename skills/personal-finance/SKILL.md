# Personal Finance (budget, debt, savings, projections)

## Order of operations

Most household plans fail from sequencing, not math. The conventional priority stack:

1. Minimum payments on all debts (never miss — late fees and credit damage compound).
2. Starter emergency buffer (~1 month of essential expenses).
3. Any employer retirement match — it's an instant 50–100% return; skipping it is the costliest
   common error.
4. High-interest debt (roughly, anything above what investments plausibly return — ~7–8%+).
5. Full emergency fund (below).
6. Long-term investing / retirement, then medium-term goals via sinking funds.

## The budget

50/30/20 (needs/wants/savings+debt-paydown, of **after-tax** income) is a rule of thumb for a
first draft, not a law — high-cost cities break the 50, high earners should push the 20 far higher.
What matters is that the budget is built from 2–3 months of *actual* transaction history, not
aspirational categories, and that every unit of income has a destination (zero-based). A budget
that ignores irregular expenses is the standard failure: annual insurance, car repairs, gifts,
travel — fund them monthly as **sinking funds** (annual cost ÷ 12) so they stop being "surprises".

```
savings rate = (income − all spending) / after-tax income     ← the single strongest lever
```

Track savings rate monthly; it predicts financial outcomes better than investment returns do.

## Emergency fund

3–6 months of **essential** expenses (not income) is the convention; push toward 6–12 for
single-income households, variable/freelance income, or shaky job markets. Held liquid (high-yield
savings), never invested — its job is availability, not return. Sizing it on gross income
overshoots; sizing it on "what I could cut to" is honest.

## Debt payoff

- **Avalanche**: extra payments to the highest interest rate first. Mathematically optimal.
- **Snowball**: smallest balance first. Costs more in interest, buys quick wins and adherence.

The best method is the one the person actually sustains — an avalanche abandoned in month three
loses to a snowball completed. Either way: list every debt with balance, rate, minimum; fix a
total monthly payoff amount; never redirect it when a debt clears (roll it into the next).
Consolidation only helps if the rate drops AND the freed-up credit isn't re-spent.

## Compound growth and projections

```
FV = P(1+r)^n + PMT × [((1+r)^n − 1) / r]      (lump sum + regular contributions, r per period)
Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 / annual return %
```

Projection discipline:
- Use **real** (inflation-adjusted) returns for long horizons — e.g. a nominal 7–10% equity
  assumption becomes ~4–7% real. Mixing nominal returns with today's expenses overstates outcomes.
- Show a range (e.g. 3%/5%/7% real), never a single path; sequence-of-returns risk means the
  average return is not the experienced return, especially near retirement.
- Retirement sizing rule of thumb: target ≈ 25× annual spending (the inverse of the ~4% initial
  withdrawal rate guideline). It's a planning anchor, not a guarantee — revisit with horizon and
  local tax/pension context.

## Net worth and insurance

Net worth = everything owned − everything owed, tracked monthly/quarterly on the same date. The
trend is the signal; the level is context. Insurance is for **catastrophic, can't-self-fund**
risks: term life (only if someone depends on the income — a common sizing convention is ~10×
income or a needs-based calculation), health, disability (the most under-bought), liability.
Insurance as investment (whole life for savings) is usually a poor trade — buy protection and
invest the difference, absent a specific tax/estate reason.

## Failure modes

- Budgeting aspirationally instead of from actual transaction history — blown by month two.
- No sinking funds, so every annual bill "breaks" the budget and gets charged to credit.
- Investing the emergency fund, then selling at a loss during the exact emergency it was for.
- Paying extra on a 3% mortgage while carrying 24% card debt.
- Projecting at nominal returns against today's expenses (inflation omitted).
- Lifestyle inflation absorbing every raise — savings rate flat while income doubles.
- Counting illiquid or optimistic asset values (car at purchase price) in net worth.

## Reviewer checklist

- [ ] Budget built from ≥2 months of real transactions; every income unit assigned
- [ ] Sinking funds for all irregular/annual expenses
- [ ] Emergency fund sized on essential expenses, held liquid, months stated
- [ ] Debt list complete (balance/rate/minimum); payoff order and method explicit
- [ ] Employer match captured before extra debt paydown beyond high-interest
- [ ] Projections in real terms, shown as a range, assumptions stated
- [ ] Savings rate computed and tracked over time

## Provenance

Standard personal-finance practice. 50/30/20, 3–6 months, 25×/4%, 10× income, and the return
assumptions are all **rules of thumb** — anchors for a first plan, to be adjusted for jurisdiction
(taxes, pensions, healthcare) and the household's actual risk tolerance. Not financial advice.
